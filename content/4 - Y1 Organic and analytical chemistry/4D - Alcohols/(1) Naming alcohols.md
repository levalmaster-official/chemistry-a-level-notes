Alcohols are compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms in an alkane have been replaced by an $\ce{-OH}$ group. Alcohols are named with the suffix "-ol".  When there are two hydroxyl groups on the molecule, it takes the suffix "-diol" and an "e" is added to the parent chain name.

```smiles
CC(O)CC(C)C
CC(O)CC(C)C(=O)CO
```
*4-methylpentan-2-ol and 4-hydroxy-2-methylpentanoic acid*

>[!NOTE] Priority groups
>According to IUPAC naming of organic compounds, there is a priority order to all functional groups:
>**Carboxylic acid, esters, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, amines, alkenes, halogens, alkyl**
>
#### Classifying alcohols
Alcohols can be classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary.

| Primary (1º)                                                                     | Secondary (2º)                                                                   | Tertiary (3º)                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hydroxyl group is on a carbon attached to one alkyl group and two hydrogen atoms | Hydroxyl group is on a carbon attached to two alkyl groups and one hydrogen atom | Hydroxyl group is on a carbon attached to three alkyl groups and no hydrogen atoms |
| `$smiles=CCCCCO`                                                                 | `$smiles=CC(O)CCC`                                                               | `$smiles=CC(C)(O)CC`                                                               |
### Physical properties of alcohols
#### Melting and boiling points
Alcohols tend to have higher melting and boiling points, and are more soluble than their corresponding [[(1) Alkanes|alkanes]].

Alcohols are [[(4) Electronegativity#Bond polarity|polar]] molecules as they have a polar $\ce{O-H}$ bonds, which can [[(5) Intermolecular forces#Hydrogen bonding|hydrogen]] bond to neighbouring molecules. They also have [[(5) Intermolecular forces#Induced dipole-dipole interactions|London forces]] acting between molecules. The overall strength of these intermolecular forces is greater, and require more energy to break.

However, these differences become smaller as their chain length increases. As chain length increases, the $\ce{-OH}$ becomes an increasingly small part of the molecule, so the  contribution of the hydrogen bonding to the overall strength of the intermolecular forces diminishes, and the melting/boiling points become more comparable.

>[!QUESTION] Why would we want to include the oxygen on the alcohol for a fair comparison of chain lengths?
>So that the number of atoms and electrons each molecule has are comparable

#### Solubility
A compound that can form hydrogen bonds with water will be more soluble than a compound that cannot.

Alkanes are non-polar and cannot H-bond, and so have poor solubility. Short-chain alcohols like methanol or ethanol are completely soluble in water as they can hydrogen bond.

As the hydrocarbon chain length increases on the alcohol, the influence of the –OH group decreases, and the longer chain alcohols behave more and more similarly to alkanes so solubility decreases. Examples: Propan-1-ol is miscible with water. Octan-1-ol is barely soluble in water – 0.3 g may dissolve in a litre of water. Once the chain length is 18-20 carbons long, it is basically insoluble; only a couple of micrograms may dissolve in a litre of water.
