Specification reference: [[OCR A Chemistry Specification#Reactions of alkanes|4.1.2 Alkanes]]

Alkanes have strong bonds with very low polarity, and therefore they have very high bond enthalpy (large activation energy) and do not attract attacking species like ions or polar molecules very easily. Therefore, alkanes are generally very **unreactive**.

However, if the activation energy can be overcome, alkanes can react in a limited number of ways:
1. [[#Combustion|Combustion reactions]]
2. [[#Halogenation|Halogenation reactions]]

##### Combustion
In a plentiful supply of oxygen, alkanes burn cleanly producing a blue flame. All the carbon atoms are oxidised to carbon dioxide, and all the hydrogen to water. This is known as **complete combustion**.

For example, the combustion of ethane:
$$\ce{C2H6 + 3 \frac{1}{2}O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$$
If the supply of oxygen is limited, a yellow flame is seen, Instead of carbon dioxide, the carbon may form carbon monoxide, or carbon (soot). All the hydrogen atoms still oxidise to water. This is known as **incomplete combustion**.

For example, the combustion of ethane to form carbon monoxide:
$$\ce{C2H6 + 2 \frac{1}{2} O2 -> 2CO + 3H2O}$$
###### Problems with incomplete combustion
1. Both carbon monoxide and particulate carbon (soot) are dangerous to human health
2. Carbon monoxide is **toxic** - it binds tightly to haemoglobin instead of oxygen, preventing oxygen from being transported in the blood to respiring tissues
3. Soot can cause respiratory issues
4. The soot from incomplete combustion also stains and damages.

##### Halogenation
Alkanes react with halogens to produce (mono)haloalkanes and hydrogen halides. This is known as a **substitution** reaction. E.g. the reaction of methane with bromine:
$$
\ce{CH4(g) +Br2(g) ->[UV light] CH3Br(g) + HBr(g)}
$$
>[!INFO] Substitution reaction
>A reaction in which an atom or group of atoms is replaced with another atom or groups of atoms
>- In the above reaction, a H atom is replaced with a Br atom.

This reaction only works in the presence of high energy UV light. This is needed to break bonds in the halogen molecules. 

It occurs by **radical substitution**, including a method involving [[(5) Reaction mechanisms#Homolytic fission|homolytic fission]]. The mechanism occurs in three stages: initiation, propagation, and termination.

###### Initiation
The $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond in $\ce{Br2}$ is broken [[(5) Reaction mechanisms#Homolytic fission|homolytically]] (UV radiation provides the energy for fission)
$$\ce{Br2 -> Br^{.} + Br^{.}}$$
In initiation, a non-radical is split into radicals, so radicals only appear on the right hand side of the equation.
###### Propagation
1. The methane reacts with a bromine radical, producing a methyl radical: $\ce{Br^{.} + CH4 -> HBr + CH3^{.}}$. In this step, a single atom from the organic reactant becomes attached to the radical, leaving behind the rest of the molecule as a radical.
2. The methyl radical then reacts with a bromine molecule forming $\ce{CH3Br}$ and remaking the bromine radical: $\ce{CH3^{.} + Br2 -> CH3Br + Br^{.}}$ . In this step, the bromine radical is regenerated, allowing the propagation stage to be repeated in a chain reaction.
###### Termination
Radicals are only removed from the reaction when they collide with another radical.
$$\ce{CH3^{.} + CH3^{.} -> C2H6}$$
$$\ce{CH3^{.} + Br^{.} -> CH3Br}$$
$$\ce{Br^{.} + Br^{.} -> Br2}$$
As the radical concentration is normally much lower than the organic molecule concentration, this stage has a low likelihood of occurring. In termination, radicals join the make non-radicals, so radicals only appear on the left hand side of the equation.

##### Problems that can occur in halogenation reactions
- Oversubstitution - the reaction will not stop until all the hydrogens have been substituted
- Substitution on the wrong carbon - forms multiple products
