### Reactivity
Group 2 metals have an outer shell $\ce{s^{2}}$ electron configuration. They lose two electrons in redox reactions to form 2+ ions. 
#### Relative reactions of Group 2 elements
#### With oxygen
Group 2 metals react with oxygen in the following reaction:
$$\ce{2M(s) + O_{2}(g) -> 2MO(s)}$$
#### With water
Group 2 metals react with water to form alkaline hydroxides. More vigorous bubbling can be seen down the group, which shows the relative reactivities of the Group 2 elements.
$$\ce{M(s) + 2H2O(l) -> M(OH)2(aq) + H_{2}(g)}$$
#### With acids
Group 2 metals react with acids readily, such as in the following reaction with sulfuric acid:
$$\ce{M(s) + H_{2}SO_{4}(aq)-> MSO_{4}(aq) + H_{2}(g)}$$
#### Trend in reactivity down Group 2
When Group 2 metals react, they are oxidised, losing two electrons ($\ce{M -> M^{2+} + 2e-}$). Hence, the less energy required to remove electrons, the more reactive a given electron will be. This energy is given by the sum of the first and second [[(3) Ionisation energy|ionisation energies]].

Ionisation energy is determined by the following factors (which affect nuclear attraction):
- Nuclear charge (increases down group 2)
- Atomic radius (increases down group 2)
- Shielding (increases down group 2)

Overall, nuclear attraction to valence electrons decreases down the group, resulting in lower ionisation energies, and increased ease of losing electrons.
### Reactions of Group 2 compounds
Group 2 oxides (like most metal oxides) are [[(1) Acids, bases and alkalis|bases]]. They are also, at least partially, soluble in water:
$$\ce{MO(s) + H_{2}O(l) -> M(OH)2(aq) }$$
When a metal oxide dissolves in water, aqueous oxide ions forms initially, These immediately react with the water to form hydroxide ions.
$$\ce{MO(s) + H_{2}O(l) -> M^{2+}(aq) + O^{2-}(aq) + H_{2}O(l) -> M^{2+}(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$$
Therefore, group 2 metal oxides are also alkalis. The solubility of Gr 2 oxides (and hydroxides) **increases** down the group. This results in a greater concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ ions in solution and hence a higher alkalinity and pH. Typically, a solution of $\ce{MgO}$ has a pH of about <span style="color:lightblue">9-10</span> whereas one of $\ce{BaO}$ (much more soluble) may have a pH of about <span style="color:orchid">11-12</span>.

Since Group 2 compounds are bases, they have various uses, including:
- $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ in agriculture to neutralise acid soils
- $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ and $\ce{CaCO_{3}}$ as 'antacids' in treating indigestion
