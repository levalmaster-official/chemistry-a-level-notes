Specification reference: [[OCR A Chemistry Specification#^8f4341|Determination of enthalpy changes]]

Calorimetry is a type of experimental technique used to measure heat energy exchanged during chemical reactions. In the lab, this is achieved by measuring the heat transferred between a chemical reaction and a sample of water. There are two methods, depending on whether the reaction occurs in aqueous solution or for other reactions, e.g. combustion.
##### Calorimetry methods
Method 1 - for reactions occurring in aqueous solution
1. Carry out the reaction using a known mass of reactant
2. Use known volume of water to absorb/provide energy
3. Measure temperature change

Method 2 - for other reactions
1. Burn a known amount of reactant
2. Use a known volume of water to absorb the energy
3. Measure temperature change.
#### Relationship between enthalpy and temperature
Energy changes of reactions can be calculated using the following equation:
$$
q = mc\Delta T
$$
This is expressed in terms of energy exchanged with the surroundings ($q$, in J), mass of substance being heated or cooled ($m$, in g), specific heat capacity of the substance ($c$, in $J g^{-1}C^{-1}$), and the temperature change ($\Delta T$, in ºC).

>[!NOTE] Standard assumptions
>In most cases, the substance being heated or cooled is water.  The specific heat capacity of water is $\ce{4.18 K g^{-1} K^{-1}}$.  If the density of the solution is not given, you can assume it is $\ce{1.00 g cm^{-1}}$.

Enthalpy change ($\ce{\Delta H}$) is in units of $\ce{kJ mol^{-1}}$ . Therefore, the energy exchange, $q$ (in kJ) must be divided by the number of moles of reactants.
$$
\Delta H = - \frac{q}{mol}
$$
>[!QUESTION] Why is a negative sign before $\frac{q}{mol}$?
>Enthalpy change measured the change in energy of the **products** from the reactants. If energy is transferred into the surroundings, the energy must be transferred *out of* the reactants. Therefore, the energy change that we measure is the opposite of the internal enthalpy change.
